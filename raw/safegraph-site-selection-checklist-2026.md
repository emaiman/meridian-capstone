## The 10-step checklist

1. **Map the current store landscape.** Plot all stores with revenue, lease expiration, and trade areas. Find white space, overlaps, and performance clusters. Use several trade-area methods (radius, drive time, catchment modeling). Example: two coffee locations nine minutes apart had 22% visitor overlap. Mistake: straight-line distance instead of drive or walk time.
2. **Understand how other brands affect your stores.** Map competitors and complementary businesses; compare performance near competitors vs. in isolation. Example: apparel stores within a quarter mile of grocery anchors outperformed the chain average by 18%. Mistake: assuming competitor proximity is always bad.
3. **Locate the target demographic.** Overlay age, income, household data on the store footprint; use mobility data for actual visitor origins; profile top stores' catchments. Example: a pet supply chain's best stores drew renters aged 25 to 34, not the assumed high-income suburbanites. Mistake: scoring on population density alone.
4. **Enrich with physical site data.** Visibility, parking, ingress and egress, traffic signals, building type, transit. Weight by format (grocery needs generous parking). Mistake: judging from satellite imagery alone.
5. **Evaluate financial and legal fundamentals.** Rent, CAM/NNN, build-out, permitting, zoning. Mistake: negotiating a lease before confirming zoning and permits. Example: a restaurant group spent about $200,000 before finding zoning barred liquor licenses.
6. **Analyze current store performance.** Look beyond revenue to visit frequency, dwell time, and origins. Example: university-adjacent fitness clubs got 30% more visits per sq ft than the chain average. Mistake: relying only on trailing 12-month revenue.
7. **Find lookalike locations with AI-assisted scoring.** Build scoring models from best-performing stores. Example: a regional grocery chain screened 150 candidate sites to 12 in under a week. Mistake: trusting scores without site visits.
8. **Determine space size and layout.** Compare spaces to proven footprints. Example: a home goods retailer found 8,000 to 10,000 sq ft consistently best on sales per sq ft. Mistake: taking available space instead of the right footprint.
9. **Adjust for your vertical.** Grocery: tight trade areas (most trips within a few miles of home); parking carries outsized weight because of dwell time and basket size; competitive saturation matters more than in most verticals. QSR and apparel have different criteria. Mistake: applying one scoring template to all formats.
10. **Validate performance after opening.** Set 90-day and 12-month checkpoints; compare projected vs. actual visits and trade area capture. Example: a specialty retailer found 40% of traffic came from outside its modeled trade area. Mistake: treating opening day as the finish line.

## Grocery-specific points

- Tight trade areas: most grocery trips are within a few miles of home.
- Parking supply and layout carry "outsized weight."
- Cannibalization risk: competitive saturation analysis "matters more here than almost any other vertical, since a new entrant can quickly cannibalize an already-thin margin category."

## Key takeaway (article)

"A complete site selection process combines demographic and mobility data, competitive mapping, physical site factors, financial and legal review, and a post-opening validation loop, not just one or two of these in isolation."
